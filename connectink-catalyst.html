The Big Idea

Giving Brooklyn students a civic voice — in writing, in public.

Young people in Brooklyn are living through one of the most consequential periods of urban change in a generation: neighborhood transformation, institutional distrust, economic pressure, questions about who the city is actually for. And yet their voices are almost entirely absent from civic conversation.

This project embeds a personal narrative writing module inside Northside Charter High School's Summer Bridge program, giving students a structured and supported space to explore their relationship to New York City, its neighborhoods, its challenges, and their own place within it. It culminates in a public showcase where students read their work aloud to community members and civic leaders, including Council Member Lincoln Restler.

Writing is a civic act. A student who puts language to their experience of the city — and then shares it publicly with someone who holds power in that city — has participated in democracy in a way that lasts.

Why This Moment

An underleveraged space, at exactly the right time.

Summer Bridge programs are an underleveraged civic space. Students are present, cohort identity is forming, and there's genuine openness to experiences that feel different from the school year. Traditionally that time gets filled with academic remediation. This module proposes something complementary: reflection, authorship, and connection to place.

Northside Charter sits in North Brooklyn, a neighborhood Council Member Restler represents and one that has absorbed enormous change. The geographic and political alignment here is intentional. Instead of being a generic writing workshop, ConnectInk is a site-specific civic intervention.

What It Looks Like

Six to eight sessions. A summer. A public moment.

Facilitated sessions woven into the Bridge program schedule. Each builds on the last:

  • Early sessions Observation and memory. Students write about their block, their borough, what's changed, what they want.
  • Middle sessions Argument and civic voice. What does the city get wrong? What would they fix? Students move from personal to public.
  • Final sessions Editing, reading aloud, building comfort with being heard. Preparation for the public showcase.
  • Showcase Students read their work. Council Member Restler and community guests listen and respond. A printed zine documents their voices and stays in the neighborhood long after the summer ends.
The Civic Payoff

By the end of summer.

15–25 students will have developed a personal civic narrative and shared it publicly
1 community showcase bringing young residents into direct dialogue with an elected leader
1 printed zine distributing student voices into the neighborhood — a lasting community artifact
